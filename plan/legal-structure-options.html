Legal structure options

Not legal advice. This page compares options so the founder and future advisors can have a faster, cheaper conversation with a lawyer. Costs are government fees only, in the currency shown, checked on 2026-09-24. Items marked [UNVERIFIED] were not confirmed from an official source.

The short answer

  • Right now: stay an unincorporated open-source project that accepts no money. This is free and safe as long as no funds are held.
  • Next: create a small non-profit corporation (Quebec or federal) once there are at least a few committed co-stewards. It is cheap and quick, and it can be self-filed.
  • Then decide between two paths for real money:
    • Path A, charity: apply to become a registered charity whose first purpose is relieving poverty with cash. It can issue tax receipts, but payouts must go to people in poverty, not everyone.
    • Path B, partner: run the first real fund inside an existing organization (a partner charity, or a group like Windfall Trust) and focus E4H on design, outreach, and the open-source software.
  • Later: add other entities only when there is a clear need, for example a US "friends of" charity for US donors, or a separate company for an investment product.

The key finding: in both Canada and the US, charity law does not allow payments to everyone regardless of need. Universal payouts are a long-term goal that will need a different legal basis, such as government policy, an international agreement, or a non-charitable structure that does not offer tax receipts. That is not a reason to stop. It is a reason to start where need is greatest, which is also what Windfall Trust plans to do.

Option by option

1. Stay an unincorporated open-source project (now)

  • What it allows: publishing ideas, code, and documents; talking to partners; building prototypes with pretend data.
  • What it does not allow: safely holding anyone else's money. Money in a personal account mixes personal and project funds, has no legal protection for the founder, and gives donors no accountability.
  • Rule for this stage: do not accept donations, crypto, or "pledges" of any kind. The site already says this, and it should keep saying it.

2. Fiscal sponsorship

A fiscal sponsor is an existing organization that "adopts" a project so it can use the sponsor's bank account, legal status, and sometimes tax receipts.

  • In Canada the rules are strict. A registered charity can only host a project as its own program. It must "own" and control the project and its funds. It cannot simply pass money through. (Metcalf Foundation, Shared Platforms; CRA, using an intermediary)
  • Open Collective is software for transparent budgets, plus a network of hosts. The US charity host Open Collective Foundation closed on 31 December 2024 (announcement). Open Source Collective still operates, charges 10% of incoming funds, only hosts open-source software projects, is a US 501(c)(6) (gifts are not tax-deductible), holds funds only in US dollars, and cannot pay people in sanctioned countries (OSC projects page; is OSC right for me).
  • Fit for E4H: good for funding the software and operations (Bucket B in the administration principles). Poor fit for the Humanity Fund itself, because hosts rarely allow investing donations long-term or paying individuals worldwide.
  • Lesson to copy: Open Collective's public ledger, where every income and expense is visible.

3. Quebec non-profit (OBNL, Part III of the Companies Act)

  • Cost: $199 for letters patent; $27 optional name reservation; $41 annual registration fee in 2026; the annual update declaration is free if filed on time. (Registraire des entreprises fees; 2026 fee schedule RE-101)
  • After filing: an initial declaration is due within 60 days, free if on time (Constituer une PMSBL).
  • People needed: Part III normally needs at least three applicants [UNVERIFIED]. This is a good forcing function: E4H should not be a one-person organization anyway.
  • Pros: cheapest, local, French-language home, simple.
  • Cons: cannot issue tax receipts. Operating mainly in French with the Quebec registry.

4. Federal non-profit (CNCA)

  • Cost and time: $200 online, usually 1 day; annual return $12 (Corporations Canada fees; annual return).
  • Extra step: a federal corporation based in Quebec must also register with the Quebec registry and pay its annual fee [UNVERIFIED exact fee].
  • Pros: recognized across Canada, English or French, well-documented by-law templates.
  • Cons: two registries to keep current.

What a non-profit (NPO) can and cannot do with money

Both non-profit options above are usually tax-exempt under the Income Tax Act, paragraph 149(1)(l). Three limits matter for E4H:

  • No personal benefit to members. No income may be paid to or available for the personal benefit of members or directors (Income Tax Act s.149). Paying the public (non-members) is not directly banned, but it is untested for a program like E4H [UNCERTAIN].
  • Investment income is a warning sign. CRA says incidental investment income is fine, but a surplus "well beyond what is reasonably required" or aggressively pursuing investment income may show a profit purpose and cost the exemption (CRA interpretation 2024-1045401E5, via Tax Interpretations). An NPO whose main activity is growing an investment fund is exactly the risky case. Get advice before an NPO holds a growing fund.
  • Filing: the NPO information return (T1044) is required if passive income is over $10,000 or assets over $200,000. Proposed rules starting in 2027 would add a short return for receipts over $10,000 and the full T1044 over $100,000 (Carters summary).

5. Canadian registered charity

  • How: an existing entity (usually a non-profit corporation) applies online through CRA My Business Account. No fee. CRA's standard is an initial response within six months of a complete application; it met that 96% of the time in 2023-24. The average time to a final decision was 147 days in 2022-23. (CRA service standards; CanadianCharityLaw.ca summary; how to apply)
  • The big limit, relief of poverty: CRA guidance CG-029 says a poverty charity must benefit people who are experiencing poverty now. Preventing poverty, or helping people "at risk", does not count. Giving more than needed to relieve poverty can be an unacceptable private benefit. Some benefit to non-poor people is allowed only if it is incidental, necessary, reasonable, and proportionate (for example, a school meal program open to all to avoid stigma). (CG-029; CRA relief of poverty page)
  • What that means for E4H: a charity can pay cash to people in poverty, in Canada or abroad, with records showing how recipients were selected. It cannot pay a universal dividend to everyone. A "universal by right, opt-in" design where the non-poor mostly let their share recycle is creative, but it is unlikely to satisfy CRA without selection criteria [UNCERTAIN; ask a charity lawyer].
  • Working outside Canada: a charity may run its own activities abroad through intermediaries it directs and controls (guidance CG-002), or, since 2022, make "qualifying disbursements" (grants) to non-qualified donees with due diligence and reporting (guidance CG-032). (CG-002; CG-032)
  • Minimum spending (disbursement quota): each year a charity must spend at least 3.5% of the average value of its investment assets up to $1 million, and 5% above $1 million. For charitable organizations it applies once those assets average more than $100,000 (CRA disbursement quota). This clashes with E4H's dynamic payout rule, which pays 0% in bad years. The rule would need a floor, or spending on charitable activities, or use of past excess spending, to stay compliant.
  • Upkeep: annual T3010 information return. In Quebec, charities also file with Revenu Québec [UNVERIFIED form number].

6. BC benefit company and community contribution company (C3)

  • Benefit company: a normal for-profit company that commits to public benefits and must publish an annual benefit report against a third-party standard. $350 to incorporate, $30 for a name, $43.39 annual report (BC benefit company guide; BC forms and fees).
  • C3: a for-profit company with dividends capped at 40% of annual profit; it cannot receive tax-deductible donations and pays tax (BC incorporated companies).
  • Fit for E4H: only for a future investment arm (the "invest for yourself" idea in the concept brief). A Quebec-based company would also need Quebec registration.

7. Trust

A trust holds property for a purpose under a trustee. In Quebec a "social trust" (fiducie d'utilité sociale) is possible under the Civil Code; elsewhere, a charitable trust. A charitable trust faces the same poverty and private-benefit limits as any charity. Trusts need careful drafting. Not researched in depth; lawyer needed.

8. United States options

  • 501(c)(3) public charity: IRS user fee $600 for Form 1023, $275 for the short Form 1023-EZ if eligible (Form 1023 instructions; 1023-EZ instructions). Grants to individuals must serve a "charitable class" chosen by objective, consistent criteria; payments to specific or pre-selected people are private benefit (1023-EZ instructions). Like Canada, universal payments to everyone would not qualify. Canadian donors generally cannot deduct gifts to US charities except against US-source income, and the reverse is also true (IRS, Canadian charities under the treaty).
  • 501(c)(4): allows advocacy; gifts not deductible.
  • Delaware public benefit corporation (PBC): $109 to incorporate; $50 annual report plus franchise tax (minimum $175 or $400 depending on method); registered agent typically $50 to $300 per year (Delaware fee schedule; Delaware franchise tax).
  • When a US entity makes sense: only once there is meaningful US donor interest. A common pattern is a US "friends of" 501(c)(3) that makes grants to the Canadian charity. That needs a lawyer.

9. Hybrids

The best-known current example is OpenAI: the nonprofit OpenAI Foundation controls a for-profit OpenAI Group PBC (OpenAI Foundation update). The lesson for E4H is that a mission-controlled parent can own a business arm. It is also expensive and complex. E4H should not consider a hybrid until there is a real business line and paid professional help.

Where to spend on a lawyer

Self-filing is fine for: the non-profit incorporation, annual returns, and a business number. A lawyer is genuinely worth it for:

  1. Charitable purposes and by-laws before applying to CRA (one review, a few hours).
  2. Payout design: the disbursement quota, the poverty-selection criteria, and the dynamic payout rule.
  3. Payments and securities wording before any public launch.
  4. Any US entity.

Low-cost routes to try first: pro bono charity-law programs, law school clinics, and advice through partner organizations [UNVERIFIED which programs serve Quebec]. Agents can prepare a short brief so paid time is used efficiently.
