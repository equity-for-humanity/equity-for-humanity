# Equity for Humanity — Global Human-Flourishing Index Research Notes v0.1

**Status:** Research for founder and governance discussion — **not** investment advice, a securities offering, a fund mandate, or a claim that a future index will outperform.

**Research date:** 9 July 2026
**Purpose:** Establish an evidence base for a simple, global, all-cap public-equity reference framework that preserves broad ownership of productive businesses while applying transparent, limited safeguards for human flourishing.

---

## 1. Short answer

**FTSE Global All Cap is an excellent starting reference universe for Equity for Humanity.** It is not objectively the one perfect global index, but it matches the mission unusually well: public companies across developed and emerging markets, large/mid/small-cap exposure, investability rules, free-float adjustment, and a mechanism for new listings to enter.

The closest serious alternative is **MSCI ACWI IMI**. It is similarly broad and investable. For this purpose, the difference between these two high-quality universes matters *far less* than keeping costs low, staying globally diversified, avoiding concentrated bets, and using transparent governance for any values-based guardrails.

The recommended design implication is therefore:

> **Use FTSE Global All Cap as the v0.1 reference universe; use MSCI ACWI IMI as the principal cross-check/fallback universe; do not build the core around forecasts of which companies will grow fastest.**

---

## 2. What is knowable — and what is not

### What broad indexing can do

A broad, market-capitalization-weighted index automatically increases exposure to businesses that become larger and decreases exposure to businesses that shrink, subject to its published eligibility and review rules. It does **not** need an investment committee to guess every future winner in advance.

That matters because long-run equity wealth creation has historically been highly concentrated. Hendrik Bessembinder's U.S. historical-stock research found that a small minority of stocks accounted for the net wealth created above Treasury bills; the often-cited result is that roughly **4%** of U.S. stocks explained the net wealth creation in the sample. This is evidence about a historical U.S. sample — not a prediction, not a guarantee, and not a reason to chase whichever names recently rose fastest.

**Practical inference:** a broad global index gives the fund a better chance of *owning* rare future outliers than a narrow “only today’s winners” screen. It cannot ensure that it owns every winner, and it will also own businesses that later disappoint.

### What cannot be honestly promised

No index can reliably identify all future high-growth companies, all future ethical leaders, or a portfolio that only rises. A profitability, quality, growth, or ESG filter can improve some characteristics, but it can also:

- exclude early-stage innovators before their financial results appear;
- increase turnover, fees, taxable events, and implementation complexity;
- create country/sector/style biases;
- depend on incomplete or inconsistent company data; and
- underperform the broad market for long periods.

For Equity for Humanity, **the core should not claim to be a forecast engine**. Its job is broad ownership. Mission values belong in a clear, narrow safeguard and stewardship layer.

## 3. Benchmark-universe comparison

| Question | FTSE Global All Cap | MSCI ACWI IMI | Meaning for E4H |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coverage goal | Large, mid and small-cap segments within the FTSE Global Equity Index Series (GEIS). | Large, mid and small-cap representation across developed and emerging markets. | Both meet the core all-cap/global design goal. |
| Markets in current source | GEIS covers securities in **49 countries** split between developed, advanced emerging and secondary emerging segments. | The 30 June 2026 factsheet states **23 developed** and **24 emerging** markets. | “Worldwide” should mean globally diversified **investable public markets**, not literally every country, private company, or frontier market. |
| Current breadth in source | FTSE rules define an index universe from the top **98%** of a regional universe by full market capitalization, subject to investability screens. | Factsheet reports **8,195 constituents** and about **99%** of the global equity investment opportunity set. | Both are broad enough that governance and implementation are more consequential than small coverage differences. |
| Weighting/investability | Adjusts constituents for free float and foreign-ownership limits; eligible securities also face voting-rights, liquidity, and market screens. | Factsheet is for a broad investable-market index; its exact methodology should be licensed/consulted before implementation. | Investability rules are a feature, not a failure: the fund needs securities that can be held, priced, and traded. |
| New companies | Semi-annual reviews; quarterly review mechanics and fast-entry rules for eligible IPOs/new issues. | The standard MSCI IMI methodology is designed to maintain investable-market coverage; confirm current methodology/license before use. | New public winners can enter by published rules; the fund will not own private companies before they list. |
| Practical conclusion | Recommended **v0.1 reference universe**, especially because it aligns with Equity for Humanity's existing concept brief. | Strong verification benchmark/fallback; not an inferior option. | Do not treat provider choice as a moral or return forecast. |

### Detail from the official FTSE rules

The July 2026 FTSE Global Equity Index Series Ground Rules say that the series covers 49 countries and includes the **FTSE Global All Cap Index Series (large, mid and small cap)**. Eligible securities are adjusted for free float and foreign ownership limits. At periodic review, FTSE defines the index universe from the top 98% of regional full market capitalization and uses buffers intended to reduce turnover. Regional reviews are generally semi-annual (March and September); specified quarterly mechanics apply to additions and adjustments.

### Detail from the official MSCI factsheet

The MSCI ACWI IMI factsheet dated 30 June 2026 says the index captures large-, mid-, and small-cap representation across 23 developed and 24 emerging markets. It reports 8,195 constituents and approximately 99% coverage of the global equity investment opportunity set. Its current country and sector composition is *not* a forecast: for example, the factsheet shows a large U.S. weight and substantial information-technology exposure, which reflects current market capitalization rather than an affirmative E4H choice.

## 4. Why “quality” should be a monitor first, not a core exclusion rule

It is reasonable to care whether businesses are financially durable, create useful products/services, respect people, and manage serious external harms. But using one composite score to decide that a company is “good” creates false precision.

A major academic study of ESG ratings found material divergence across prominent rating agencies. It attributes the divergence mainly to differences in measurement and scope, with weights contributing as well. That does not prove all ESG research is useless. It does mean that **a vendor score must never be treated as a moral truth or a mechanical guarantee of social benefit**.

A better v0.1 posture is:

1. **Keep the core broad and market-weighted** so it retains exposure to future outliers across sectors, countries, and size bands.
2. **Use a narrow set of published, contestable hard safeguards** for severe, well-defined harm or legal/integrity failure.
3. **Use ownership for stewardship** when engagement is more mission-consistent than selling.
4. **Publish a separate human-flourishing scorecard** for transparency and research; do not use it to secretly reweight the core.
5. Test any later quality/positive-impact tilt against the broad benchmark in a paper index before real capital is affected.

## 5. Human-flourishing source material

The UN Global Compact's Ten Principles provide a widely recognized minimum-responsibility frame in four areas:

- human rights;
- labour;
- environment; and
- anti-corruption.

They are useful as a **floor for a conduct policy**, not as proof that every participating or non-participating company is beneficial. A future E4H policy would still need an explicit evidence standard, independent review, remediation path, appeal path, and a decision about when a breach is severe and persistent enough to justify exclusion.

Stewardship is also important. If a broadly held company has remediable problems, retaining ownership can preserve the ability to vote, engage, file or support shareholder proposals where lawful, and demand disclosure. Exclusion remains appropriate for a narrow set of severe or irreconcilable cases, but it should not become an unreviewable black box.

---

## 6. Research conclusion for the proposal

The strongest simple model is not “find only the good, fast-growing stocks.” It is:

> **Own the broad productive public economy; let published market rules bring in emerging winners; put a small number of clearly defined humanity safeguards around the ownership; and make every exception, data source, vote, and change auditable.**

This is a governance choice, not a prediction of superior returns.
